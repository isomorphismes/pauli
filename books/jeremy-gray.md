# Jeremy Gray — *Change and Variations*

Full title: *Change and Variations: A History of Differential Equations to 1900*.

Sources:

- https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-030-70575-6
- https://books.google.com/books?id=oKkxEAAAQBAJ

Gray is a major style reference for `pauli` because he follows problems, failed methods, changing notions of solution, and the interaction of mathematics with mechanics and physics. The book is explicitly concerned with the practical roots of results that a modern analysis text can make look detached from the problems that produced them.

## The Gray order

For `pauli`, borrow this order:

1. state the physical or mathematical problem;
2. say what methods were available;
3. identify what failed or remained unclear;
4. show the method that worked;
5. introduce the concept that later made the success intelligible;
6. only then give the compressed modern formulation.

History should explain why the mathematics has the form it does. It should not be trivia attached after the formulas.

## Chapter map

The following are concise project notes, not substitutes for the book.

1. **First ordinary differential equations** — start from concrete changing quantities and the relations imposed on them, not from today's classification scheme.
2. **Early variational problems** — let optimization problems create the need for varying an entire curve or function.
3. **Vibrating strings** — use the dispute over strings to show that “function” and “solution” were themselves changing ideas.
4. **Rational mechanics** — keep differential equations attached to the mechanical questions that generated them.
5. **Early PDE theory** — show several competing methods before presenting a clean modern taxonomy.
6. **Lagrange's general PDE theory** — let generalization answer repetitions already visible in particular problems.
7. **Calculus of variations revisited** — return to an earlier topic after the language has improved; important ideas should be met more than once.
8. **Monge and PDEs** — show how geometry can reorganize a differential-equation problem rather than merely illustrate it.
9. **Revision** — pause and synthesize; recap is part of the mathematical work.
10. **Heat equation** — connect the PDE, boundary conditions, Fourier expansions, and changing ideas of admissible functions.
11. **Gauss and the hypergeometric equation** — introduce a special equation through the problems and transformations that made it recur.
12. **Existence theorems** — distinguish finding a formula from proving that a solution exists.
13. **Riemann and complex functions** — show a new mathematical language changing what can be asked about old equations.
14. **Riemann and the hypergeometric equation** — revisit a known equation from the stronger complex-analytic viewpoint.
15. **Schwarz and the hypergeometric equation** — emphasize transformations and relations among solutions, not a table of named formulas.
16. **Poincaré and complex ODEs** — shift from explicit solution formulas toward global and qualitative behavior.
17. **More general PDEs** — broaden only after enough specific examples exist to make the general questions meaningful.
18. **Green functions and Dirichlet's principle** — introduce integral and variational tools as answers to source and boundary problems.
19. **Attempts on Laplace's equation** — failed and partial methods are explanatory evidence; later rigor should be motivated by the exact gap.
20. **Applied wave equations** — keep physical applications inside the development of the mathematics, not in an end-of-chapter box.
21. **Revision** — state what the reader can now see that was invisible ten chapters earlier.
22. **Riemann on shock waves** — use a close reading of one paper to expose mathematical practice instead of replacing it with a modern summary.
23. **Minimal surfaces** — let one geometric object connect PDEs, geometry, and variational ideas.
24. **PDEs and mechanics** — after deriving equations, return to the physical system and interpret the terms.
25. **Geometrical mechanics** — geometry can be the explanation of mechanics, not an ornamental rewrite of coordinate calculations.
26. **Nineteenth-century calculus of variations** — make hidden assumptions and changing standards of rigor part of the story.
27. **Poincaré and mathematical physics** — do not respect subject boundaries that the actual mathematics and physics do not respect.
28. **Elliptic equations and regular variational problems** — ask not only whether solutions exist but what regularity the equations force.
29. **Initial data for hyperbolic PDEs** — an equation is incomplete without saying what data determine a solution and what freedom remains.
30. **Final revision** — end by organizing the conceptual changes, not merely by adding one more theorem.
31. **Translations of original sources** — separate what historical authors actually wrote from the modern interpretation placed on their work.

## Direct relevance to hydrogen

Hydrogen contains many objects that can look arbitrary when taught only as a calculation:

- separation of variables;
- Legendre and associated Legendre functions;
- Laguerre polynomials;
- boundary conditions;
- discrete eigenvalues;
- orthogonality;
- eigenfunction expansions.

A weak explanation says:

> separation gives Legendre's equation, so use associated Legendre functions.

A Gray-influenced explanation asks why regularity and boundary conditions select particular solutions, why orthogonality became useful, and which part of the derivation is merely a historical computational method.

In particular:

**separation of variables is a method; SO(3) representation theory explains what the angular spaces are.**

Do not turn a successful computational method into an ontology.

## Style rules derived from Gray

### Put the problem before the method

Do not write “we now apply separation of variables” until the reader knows what structural split the method is exploiting.

### Explain failures

A later theorem becomes intelligible when the reader knows what an earlier formal argument could not guarantee: convergence, boundary behavior, existence, uniqueness, regularity, or a legitimate operator domain.

### Mark modern reinterpretation

It is fine to say how a nineteenth-century problem looks in modern spectral language. It is not fine to imply that the original mathematician was secretly using the modern framework.

### Return to the same object

Gray revisits topics after new tools appear. `pauli` should revisit hydrogen through differential equations, symmetry, representation theory, SO(4), and computation. Each return should reveal a different structure.

### Use synthesis pages

After a dense conceptual cluster, stop and state what objects appeared, which distinctions now matter, and what remains unexplained.

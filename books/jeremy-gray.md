# Jeremy Gray — *Change and Variations*

Full title: *Change and Variations: A History of Differential Equations to 1900*.

Gray is useful here less as a source of formulas than as a guide to **why differential equations took the forms they did** and what problems made mathematicians care about them.

For `pauli`, use Gray's historical style when introducing:

- ordinary versus partial differential equations;
- heat, wave, and potential problems;
- separation of variables;
- boundary and initial conditions;
- the changing meaning of "solution";
- the emergence of spectral and eigenfunction methods.

The desired explanatory order is historical and structural:

1. what physical or mathematical problem was being studied;
2. what sort of equation arose from it;
3. what obstacle existing methods ran into;
4. what new concept or method resolved it;
5. only then the modern compact notation.

This is especially useful whenever a named equation or special function otherwise appears from nowhere.

## Separation is method, not ontology

Separation of variables matters historically and computationally. It is often how one discovers useful pieces of a solution.

But if rotational symmetry explains why the angular state decomposes into irreducible rotation types, do not pretend that the universe has spherical harmonics because somebody separated variables on a chalkboard.

Use the calculation to find or evaluate the object. Use geometry and symmetry to explain what the object is.

Gray does not make Sturm–Liouville theory a major subject of *Change and Variations* because Jesper Lützen's historical treatment of Liouville covers that development in much greater depth. See [Jesper Lützen](jesper-lutzen.md).
